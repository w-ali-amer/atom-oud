From a spoken word to a note

  1. Spoken length. A forced aligner timed each word of the recitation, from 0.10 s for the shortest to 1.18 s for the longest.
  2. Laser time. Each word switches the laser on for a time proportional to its spoken length within its line. Each line of the couplet gets exactly one full turn's worth of laser, so a word that takes a quarter of its line gets a quarter of a turn. In the timing-only version this takes the atom from ground to excited and back to ground by the end of each line; in the abjad version the changing directions leave it somewhere else. At a laser strength of 4.14 rad/µs the whole couplet is about 3.0 millionths of a second of laser.
  3. Direction. In the abjad version, each word's push comes from a direction set by the abjad value of its root, or of the word itself for small words (abjad is the old Arabic letter numbering). In the timing-only version every push comes from the same direction.
  4. Note. After each word, the chance of finding the atom excited, from 0% to 100%, is cut into eight steps of maqam Kurd on D: D, E♭, F, G, A, B♭, C, D′.
  5. Tremolo. When the atom is close to a 50/50 balance (between about 40% and 60% excited), the oud plays a quick tremolo on that note.

What is physics, and what is our choice

Physics

A resonant laser pulse turns the atom by its strength times its duration, so a word held twice as long turns the atom twice as far. The atom's state, the turns, and the noise come from simulating the atom's response to Pulser-built pulse sequences with Pasqal's published model of FRESNEL_SA1; for the noisy version, the noise terms that model leaves out are borrowed from FRESNEL_CAN1.

Only the change of direction from one word to the next matters, never the absolute direction.

Our choices

One full turn per line, the abjad directions, the eight-note scale, and the oud itself are conventions we picked. We fixed this timing rule before hearing the result.

Random directions instead of abjad give a melody this predictable about 39% of the time, so the musical shape comes from the timing, and the abjad decides which of many similar melodies you hear.

How it compares with real music

Measured against 81 pieces of Turkish makam music in the Kürdi and Kürdilihicazkar modes (the SymbTr collection), with their notes rounded to the same semitone grid. “Typical” is the median over 26-note stretches of those pieces. The atom rows are the noiseless versions.

Nine in ten real stretches have between 44% and 88% small steps and at most 12% big jumps. The abjad version sits inside that range; the timing-only version jumps a little too often.

What a real machine would see

On the hardware nobody can watch the arrow move. Each reading gives only "ground" or "excited" and ends that run, so the chance behind each note has to be estimated by running the same program many times, once for every word in the couplet. With the noise included, and allowing for the device's known readout errors, 1,000 readings per word would give the right note at least 95% of the time for 14 of the 26 words, and 5,000 readings would do so for 23. The continuous sound on this page comes from the simulation, not from a device. The simulation also treats the laser pulses as perfectly sharp; with SA1's published modulator bandwidth, which rounds the pulse edges, 3 of the 26 notes in the noiseless abjad version would come out one step different.

About the poem

The couplet and its recitation belong to a living poet, so this page leaves out the recording and the words and plays the atom's oud on its own, on the same timeline. I am happy to play the full version, with his voice, on a call.
